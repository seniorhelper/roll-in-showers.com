Quick answer

What a roll-in shower is

A roll-in shower is a curbless or near-curbless shower large enough for a person to enter seated on a shower wheelchair. The most-cited benchmark is the 2010 ADA Standards' standard roll-in: at least 30 by 60 inches inside, open across the full 60-inch side, with a threshold no higher than ½ inch.

It is different from a walk-in shower, which usually keeps a low curb, and from a transfer shower, a 36 × 36-inch stall used from a seat. Many homes use roll-in dimensions as a target even though private houses are not required to follow the ADA.

Roll-in, alternate roll-in or transfer?

Inside sizes are measured at the center points of opposing sides. Source: 2010 ADA Standards, U.S. Access Board Chapter 6.
Compartment type (2010 ADA §608.2)Clear inside sizeEntrySpace outside the shower
Transfer type (§608.2.1)36 × 36 in. exactly36 in. wide minimum on the face36 in. wide × 48 in. long minimum, measured from the control wall
Standard roll-in (§608.2.2)30 in. wide × 60 in. deep minimum60 in. wide minimum, the full long side30 in. × 60 in. minimum, alongside the open face
Alternate roll-in (§608.2.3)36 in. wide × 60 in. deep minimum36 in. wide minimum, at one end of the long sideNot set as a separate clearance in §608.2.3; plan for a wheelchair approach

Each layout fits a different way of bathing. People who stay seated in a shower wheelchair need a roll-in. People who slide from a wheelchair onto a seat can use a transfer shower or a roll-in with a folding seat. Our ADA showers guide covers seats, grab bars and controls for each type, and the transfer guide covers the move itself.

02

Who benefits from a roll-in shower

The CDC reports that more than one in four adults age 65 and older fall each year, that older-adult falls led to about 4.5 million emergency department visits in 2024, and that falls are the leading cause of injury death for this age group, with more than 43,000 deaths in 2024. Removing the tub wall or curb takes away one obstacle in the room where many people feel least steady. A roll-in shower does not prevent falls by itself; it reduces the need to step over anything and makes room for seats, bars and help.

  • Wheelchair users who need to roll in or transfer level to a seat.
  • People using walkers or rolling shower chairs, which cannot cross a curb.
  • Caregivers who need an open side to help without standing in the spray.
  • Veterans who may qualify for VA home-modification benefits.
  • Households planning ahead, since a curbless floor is hard to add later.
03

Plan it in five steps

  1. Measure the route, room and opening. Door clear width, the 30 × 60 space in front of the shower, the drain location and the floor structure.
  2. Choose how the entry goes curbless. Recessed subfloor, built-up floor or a low-profile pan.
  3. Pick a pan or kit that fits. Match the footprint and drain to your measurements, not the product name.
  4. Place the seat, bars and controls. Blocking first, then heights that suit the user.
  5. Plan the installation and quotes. Itemized scope, permits and the order of trades.
04

What a roll-in shower costs

Published national surveys give a wide range. Angi reports $2,900 to $6,600 for a standard-sized roll-in shower, Fixr puts walk-in showers at $5,000 to $11,000, and custom builds can reach $20,000. The biggest swing factors are whether the floor has to be recessed, whether the drain moves, and whether the walls are panels or tile. These are planning ranges, not our prices; your quote depends on your home. See the cost guide.

05

Paying for it

Original Medicare generally does not pay for a bathroom remodel. It does cover some equipment, such as commode chairs, when a provider orders it. Other paths include Medicaid home-modification waivers in many states, VA's HISA benefit and SAH/SHA grants for eligible veterans, USDA repair loans and grants in rural areas, and local programs found through your Area Agency on Aging. IRS Publication 502 explains when disability-related home changes can count as medical expenses; ask a tax professional. Details: funding an accessible bathroom.

Common questions

Roll-in shower questions

What is a roll-in shower?

A shower with no curb, or a beveled lip of ½ inch or less, that is large enough to enter on a shower wheelchair. The common benchmark is the ADA standard roll-in: at least 30 by 60 inches inside with a 60-inch-wide entry.

Is a roll-in shower automatically ADA compliant?

No. ADA compliance depends on the setting and the whole installed layout, including size, threshold, seat, grab bars and controls. Private homes are generally not covered by the ADA Standards, but many use the dimensions as a guide.

Can a roll-in shower replace a bathtub?

Often. Many roll-in pans are about 60 inches long to fit a tub alcove. Check the alcove size, drain location and floor structure before ordering.

How much does a roll-in shower cost?

National cost surveys report roughly $2,900 to $6,600 for a standard roll-in (Angi) and up to about $20,000 for custom builds. Your price depends on floor work, drain changes, walls and region.

Does Medicare pay for a roll-in shower?

Original Medicare generally does not pay for bathroom remodeling. Medicaid waivers, VA programs, some Medicare Advantage plans and local programs may help.

How long does installation take?

A tub-to-roll-in swap using a molded pan and panel walls is often done in one day. Floor recessing, drain moves or tile add time.
