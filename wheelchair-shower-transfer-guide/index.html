Wheelchair-to-Shower Transfers: Setting Up a Safer Move

For many wheelchair users, the riskiest part of showering is the move from chair to seat, not the shower itself. A safer transfer comes from a seat at the right height, a chair parked in the right spot, and a bar exactly where the hand goes.

Quick answer

What makes a transfer safer

A transfer is safest when the shower seat is level with the wheelchair seat, the chair can park close and at a slight angle, the person moves toward their stronger side, and there is a fixed bar to push from. Everything else—wet skin, a towel, soap—adds risk, so set the room up to make the move short and predictable.

This page explains how to plan the space and the equipment. It is not medical advice. A physical or occupational therapist should teach and check the transfer technique itself.

02

Seat height and depth

A level transfer is easier than an uphill one. Measure the height of the user's wheelchair seat cushion, then choose or mount a shower seat to match. The ADA reference for shower seats is 17 to 19 inches above the floor, measured to the top of the seat, but home installations can be set to the user. A seat that is too deep pushes the person forward; one that is too shallow leaves them perched. Most folding shower seats are about 15 to 16 inches deep.

03

Approach side and parking

Most people transfer toward their stronger side. Plan the seat so the wheelchair parks on that side, at roughly a 30 to 45 degree angle to the seat, with the brakes locked and the footrests swung away. In a roll-in shower the chair can park inside, alongside the seat. In a transfer shower it parks outside, which is why the ADA transfer layout requires a clear space 36 inches wide by 48 inches long next to the entry.

If two people may use the shower, or the stronger side might change after surgery or a stroke, a roll-in layout with a removable or portable seat keeps both sides available.

04

Grab bars that support the move

The user pushes up or pulls across during a transfer, so the bar has to be where the hand lands. In a transfer shower, the ADA layout puts a bar across the control wall and partway along the back wall. In a roll-in with a seat, bars go on the back wall and the wall opposite the seat, but not above the seat itself. A vertical bar near the entry helps many users stand or steady themselves.

Every bar needs solid blocking behind the wall. Suction-cup bars are not designed to hold body weight and should never be used for transfers.

05

Floor, water and timing

Wet floors are slippery for wheels and feet. Keep the transfer path dry: a towel ready within reach, the spray off or pointed away until the person is seated, and a floor surface with good wet traction. Our slip-resistant floors guide compares options. Lock the shower chair's casters before every transfer, and check that the brakes still hold on the wet floor surface.

06

When to stop transferring

Transfers get harder with fatigue, illness, new medications or after a fall. Warning signs include needing more than one try, sliding instead of controlled movement, or the helper taking most of the weight. Those are signals to talk with the user's doctor or therapist about a mechanical lift or a roll-in setup with a shower wheelchair. The CDC notes that falling once raises the chance of falling again, so treat a near miss as a reason to re-plan.

07

Planning checklist

  • Measure wheelchair seat height with the cushion in place.
  • Identify the stronger side and plan the parking spot on it.
  • Mark seat, bars and controls on the wall with tape before installing.
  • Confirm blocking behind every bar and seat.
  • Test the transfer dry, with the therapist if possible, before tile or panels go up.
  • Keep a towel, robe and phone within reach of the seat.

Common questions

Transfer questions

What height should a shower seat be for a wheelchair transfer?

Ideally level with the wheelchair seat so the move is sideways, not uphill. The ADA reference height for shower seats is 17 to 19 inches above the floor, measured to the top of the seat.

Which side should the wheelchair park on?

Most people transfer toward their stronger side, so plan the parking spot there. A therapist can confirm the best side for the individual.

Are transfer benches safe?

A transfer bench can work well as a temporary or low-cost option when it is used as designed, with locked legs on a level floor. It does not replace grab bars or a planned layout for long-term use.

Can I use a suction-cup grab bar for transfers?

No. Suction bars are not designed to support body weight. Use bars anchored into blocking or with anchors the manufacturer approves for the wall type.
