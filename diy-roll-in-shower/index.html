DIY Roll-In Shower: Scope, Risks and an Installation Plan

Plenty of homeowners can demolish a tub, hang wall panels and install accessories. The parts that most often fail are the ones under the pan and behind the walls. Split the job along that line.

Quick answer

The DIY split

Good DIY tasks: demolition, haul-away, setting panels on prepared walls, installing a curtain rod and storage. Usually worth hiring: structural changes to recess a floor, moving a drain, replacing the valve, and anything that needs a permit. Grab bars sit in between; they are simple to mount but only safe on correct blocking.

In one sentenceDo the visible work yourself; hire the floor, drain and valve work.See the full sequence →
01

Before you start

  • Check with your city or county whether a permit is needed for the shower, plumbing or structural changes.
  • Read the pan and wall manufacturer's installation manuals completely; warranties often depend on following them.
  • Find out what is under the floor: joist size and direction, or slab.
  • Plan how you will shower while the bathroom is out of service.
  • Line up the plumber or framer before demolition, not after.
02

Tasks that suit most DIYers

Removing a cast-iron or steel tub is heavy but straightforward with help; acrylic and fiberglass tubs can often be cut out. Removing old tile and drywall, hauling debris, installing blocking where studs are exposed, hanging panel walls with the specified adhesive, and installing the curtain rod and shelves are all within reach of a careful homeowner.

03

Tasks that often need a pro

Recessing a subfloor means changing joists, which affects the structure; get advice from someone qualified. Moving a drain affects venting and slope, and many jurisdictions require a licensed plumber and inspection. Replacing or moving a valve means soldering or crimping supply lines inside a wall that will be closed. Setting a pan on a full mortar bed is a skill; a poorly supported pan flexes and cracks.

04

The flood test

If you build a tiled or membrane-based floor, plug the drain and fill the waterproofed area with water for the time the membrane maker specifies before tiling. Mark the water line and check that it has not dropped. A leak found at this stage is a small fix; found later, it can mean tearing out the shower.

05

Stop-work triggers

  • Rot or soft subfloor around the old tub
  • Joists that were notched or cut by previous work
  • A drain line that is cracked, undersized or sloped wrong
  • Galvanized supply lines that crumble when touched
  • Any electrical wiring in the wet area
  • Signs of mold behind the walls

Stop and get the right trade in. Covering any of these up costs more later.

06

Safety for the person who will use it

A DIY shower still has to work for the user: correct seat height, blocking for every bar, a hand shower with a long hose, an anti-scald valve, and a slip-resistant floor. Use our ADA reference and hardware guide to set the numbers.

Common questions

DIY questions

Can I install a roll-in shower myself?

Many homeowners handle demolition, panels and accessories. Floor recessing, drain moves and valve work usually need a licensed trade and sometimes a permit.

Do I need a permit to replace a tub with a shower?

It depends on your jurisdiction and the scope. Plumbing or structural changes usually need one. Check with your local building department.

What is the riskiest part of a DIY roll-in shower?

Supporting the pan and getting the drain and waterproofing right. Leaks there are hidden until they cause damage.
