Roll-In Shower Pans: The Foundation of a Barrier-Free Shower

The pan decides the footprint, the drain location and how low the entry can be. Pick it after you measure, because once it is set in mortar everything else is built around it.

Quick answer

Four choices that define a pan

Every roll-in pan comes down to four choices: footprint (width × depth), drain (center, end, or trench), entry (flush, beveled low threshold, or a collapsible dam), and shape (rectangular alcove, corner, or neo-angle). The 15 pans linked from this site run from 36 × 36 inches up to 60-inch-long models, with center, trench and dual-option drains.

02

Drain options

A center drain sits in the middle of the pan and the floor slopes to it from all sides. It is the most common molded-pan layout. An end drain sits near one short side, which can line up with an old tub drain. A trench drain is a long channel along one edge, so the floor slopes in one plane. Two pans linked here list a trench drain, and one of those also offers a center-drain option.

The most practical rule: choose the pan whose drain lands closest to where your existing drain already is. Moving a drain under a wood floor is common; under a slab it means cutting concrete.

03

Entry and threshold

Pans called "barrier-free" or "roll-in" usually have a beveled low threshold or a flush entry designed to sit level with the finished floor. Some listings describe a low threshold without giving a height. For wheelchair use, ask the seller for the actual entry height and bevel and compare it to the user's chair. The ADA reference for a roll-in is ½ inch maximum. A pan described as "step in" may have a higher lip that suits walkers but not wheels.

04

Corner and neo-angle shapes

Corner pans have two finished sides and two wall sides, which can open up a bathroom and give a wider approach. Neo-angle pans clip the front corner at an angle, so the entry faces diagonally. That can make a small room feel bigger, but a diagonal entry is narrower than the pan width and harder to roll into straight. Measure the entry face itself.

05

Setting a pan correctly

Most molded pans need full support underneath, usually a mortar bed or the manufacturer's specified setting compound, on a flat, solid subfloor. Gaps under the pan cause flexing, cracking and leaks at the drain. The installer should dry-fit the pan, check the drain alignment, check level in both directions, and follow the manufacturer's sequence for connecting the drain and sealing the flanges. Ask for photos before the walls go on.

  • Confirm the threshold profile in the planned installation.
  • Confirm the drain body type and connection method.
  • Check that walls and blocking work with the flange design.
  • Keep the installation manual with your project file.

Common questions

Pan questions

What size roll-in shower pan do I need?

Start with the opening and the user. To replace a tub, most people look at 60-inch pans in 30- to 36-inch depths. For wheelchair users who stay seated, the 30 × 60-inch ADA reference is a common minimum for the clear inside space.

Is a trench drain or center drain better for a roll-in pan?

Either can work. A center drain is typical in molded pans. A trench drain allows a one-direction slope and a level entry edge. Choose the one that lines up with your drain and floor.

Can a roll-in pan sit on a concrete slab?

Yes, if the slab is flat and the drain is in the right place. For a flush entry the slab may have to be lowered in the shower area.

How high is a low-threshold pan?

It varies by model. Ask the seller for the actual entry height and bevel. The ADA reference for a roll-in is ½ inch maximum.
