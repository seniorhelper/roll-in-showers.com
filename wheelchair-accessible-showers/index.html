Layouts for people who use a wheelchair

Wheelchair-Accessible Showers Designed Around the User

A wheelchair-accessible shower works when the chair can reach it, the person can get from chair to seat or roll straight in, and every control is in reach from where they sit. This guide plans those three things in order.

Quick answer

Two ways to shower from a wheelchair

People who use wheelchairs shower in one of two ways. Some roll in on a waterproof shower wheelchair (often a shower commode chair) and stay seated the whole time. Others transfer from their everyday chair to a seat or bench in the shower. The first needs a curbless entry and a long, open footprint. The second needs a seat at the right height with a clear parking spot beside it.

Many households plan for both: a roll-in footprint with a fold-up seat, so the shower still works if the user's strength changes or a caregiver takes over more of the routine.

Talk with the user's occupational therapist if they have one. An OT can watch a transfer and recommend seat height, grab bar positions and which side the chair should approach from. That advice is more specific than any standard.

02

The approach: getting the chair to the shower

Most planning failures happen before the shower. Check that the bathroom door gives 32 inches of clear width or more, that the door swing does not trap the chair, and that the floor between the door and the shower is level with no rugs or lips.

In front of a roll-in entry, plan a clear rectangle at least 30 by 60 inches. That is where the user lines up and where a caregiver stands. If the bathroom also needs a full turn, the 60-inch turning circle can overlap the clear floor space, which is how compact accessible bathrooms fit.

03

The entry: flush, sloped, or a low lip

A flush entry, where the bathroom floor runs straight into the shower, is the easiest for casters and for a caregiver pushing a chair. A collapsible rubber water dam is a common compromise: it holds water back and folds flat when a wheel rolls over it. A molded pan with a low, beveled lip is the simplest retrofit, but even a ½-inch lip can stop a small front caster.

Whichever you choose, the floor needs to drain away from the entry. Our barrier-free shower guide explains the slope and drain choices.

04

Inside the shower: parking, reach and spray

A seated user's comfortable reach is low and to the side. Put the valve where it can be reached from the seat or chair, ideally before the water hits the person, so they can test the temperature. A hand shower on a slide bar lets the spray height change for a seated or standing user and lets a caregiver aim it.

  • Mount controls within reach of the seat: the ADA reference is no higher than 48 inches and within 27 inches of the seat wall in a standard roll-in.
  • Use a hose of at least 59 inches.
  • Choose lever handles, which need no grip or twist.
  • Add a pressure-balance or thermostatic valve to limit scalding; the ADA cap is 120°F.
  • Place soap and shampoo storage between 15 and 48 inches high so it can be reached from a seat.
05

Shower wheelchairs and commode chairs

A shower wheelchair is built to get wet, with a mesh or padded waterproof seat, rust-resistant frame and locking casters. Some are self-propelled with large rear wheels; others are attendant-propelled with four small casters. Small casters need a smoother, flatter entry. Measure the chair's overall width and length and test it on the planned floor surface if you can.

Medicare lists commode chairs as covered durable medical equipment when a doctor or other provider orders them for use in the home, so ask the user's provider before buying one. Medicare does not pay for the shower remodel itself. See paying for an accessible bathroom.

06

Room for a caregiver

When someone helps with bathing, they need to stand where they can reach the user without standing in the spray. An open-sided roll-in gives that access naturally. A 36-inch or deeper compartment, a curtain instead of glass, and a hand shower the helper can hold make assisted bathing much easier. Our caregiver-assisted showering guide covers positions, privacy and supplies.

07

Before you buy anything

Bring three things to every product or installer conversation: the user's chair measurements, a sketch of the bathroom with the route marked, and a note of whether they roll in or transfer. Then compare pans and packages against that brief. The product catalog lists the footprints currently linked from this site.

Common questions

Wheelchair shower questions

What is the best shower for a wheelchair user?

It depends on whether the person can transfer. Users who stay seated in a shower wheelchair need a roll-in shower with a flush or very low entry. Users who transfer well can use a transfer shower with a seat at the same height as their wheelchair.

How big does a wheelchair-accessible shower need to be?

A common benchmark is 30 by 60 inches inside for a roll-in shower and 36 by 36 inches for a transfer shower, from the 2010 ADA Standards. Add a clear space outside the entry for the chair to line up.

Does Medicare pay for a shower wheelchair?

Medicare lists commode chairs as covered durable medical equipment when a provider orders them for home use, and coverage depends on meeting Medicare rules. Medicare does not cover remodeling the shower itself.

Can I use glass doors on a wheelchair-accessible shower?

Fixed glass across the entry blocks the chair and a caregiver. Many accessible showers use a weighted curtain, or glass on one end only, so the open side stays clear.
