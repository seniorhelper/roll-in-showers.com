Quick answer

What are the standard shower sizes?

Common standard shower sizes are 32 x 32, 36 x 36, 48 x 36 and 48 x 48 inches for stalls, and 60 x 30 to 60 x 36 inches where a shower replaces a tub. The International Residential Code sets the floor at 900 square inches inside, with no side under 30 inches. For a seat, start at 36 x 36; for a wheelchair, 30 x 60.

Which standard shower size fits which kind of use?

Shower bases and enclosures are sold in a fairly short list of footprints, because they have to fit standard framing and the 60 inch space a bathtub leaves behind. Home-improvement size guides list the same handful again and again. What those guides rarely say is how each size behaves once someone needs to sit down, park a walker or roll in. The 36 x 36 row deserves a second look. It is the most familiar square stall, and it is also the exact inside size the 2010 ADA Standards use for a transfer shower (§608.2.1). That makes it a good choice for someone who sits to shower and can slide from a chair to a wall seat, and a poor one for someone who needs to stay in a shower wheelchair, because the chair has nowhere to go.

Area alone can mislead. A 48 x 48 corner shower has more floor than a 60 x 30 alcove, yet the long, narrow 60 x 30 is the one a wheelchair can roll straight into, because its whole long side can be open. Shape and entry matter as much as square inches.

02

What is the minimum shower size by code?

Most US homes fall under the International Residential Code or a state version of it. Section P2708.1 sets the minimum for any shower, accessible or not. Your city or county may amend it, so treat these as the starting point and confirm with the building department.

  • 900 square inches of interior floor area, and no finished interior dimension under 30 inches. Valves, showerheads, soap dishes and grab bars do not count against the measurement.
  • One narrow-shower exception: a 25 inch minimum dimension is allowed if the compartment has at least 1,300 square inches of floor area.
  • Fold-down seats: allowed in a minimum-size shower as long as the 900 square inches is still there with the seat folded up.
  • The opening: at least 22 inches of clear, unobstructed finished width to get in and out. Hinged shower doors must open outward.
  • Height: the minimum size has to hold up to at least 70 inches above the drain, so a sloped ceiling or a tile ledge cannot eat into it at head height.
  • Room in front: the IRC's fixture-clearance section asks for at least 24 inches of clear space in front of the shower opening.

Read those numbers with an older bather in mind and the gap is plain. A 22 inch opening meets code, yet the 2010 ADA Standards ask for 32 inches of clear width at doorways (§404.2.3), the figure most planners use for anyone with a mobility aid. A 30 inch square meets code, yet leaves no room for a seat and a person to share the floor.

03

Why does a standard size often fall short later?

Most showers are bought for the person who walks in today. Five years on, the same footprint has to hold a seat, a hand shower, grab bars and sometimes a second person. Four details decide whether it can.

  • Nominal is not clear. A base sold as 36 x 36 is measured at its outside edges. Once wall panels or tile go on, the clear inside can lose an inch or more each way. The ADA measures shower size at the center points of opposing finished walls, which is the honest way to measure yours.
  • The seat takes half a small stall. The ADA puts a shower seat 17 to 19 inches above the floor (§610.3), and a rectangular seat's front edge sits 15 to 16 inches out from the wall. In a 32 inch stall that leaves about 16 inches of floor beside a lowered seat.
  • The curb. Ordinary shower bases have a raised curb to hold water. The ADA roll-in limit is ½ inch (§608.7), with a 2 inch allowance only for transfer showers in existing buildings where a lower threshold would disturb the floor slab. A walker or a wheelchair wheel does not climb a standard curb gracefully.
  • Blocking. Grab bars need solid backing in the wall. Adding it while the walls are open costs little; adding it later means opening finished walls.

None of this means you need the biggest shower available. It means picking the standard size that matches the hardest day you can reasonably see coming, then building the entry and the walls to match.

Will a standard shower pan fit where your tub is now?

Usually. Standard bathtubs are about 60 inches long, so the most common replacement pans are 60 inches long and 30 to 36 inches deep. A 60 x 30 or 60 x 32 base often drops into the alcove the tub used, and a 60 x 36 needs a few extra inches of depth that some bathrooms have and some do not. Shorter 54 inch pans exist for alcoves with an unusual wall. Every pan and package on this site, from 36 x 36 up to 60 x 48, is compared in one shower pan size table.

Size is only half the fit. The drain position (left, right, center or trench) and the outlet size have to work with the plumbing under the floor; our conversion drain size guide walks through that. How do you measure a shower the right way?

  1. Measure finished wall to finished wall, at the floor and again at about shoulder height. Use the smaller number.
  2. Measure the opening you will walk or roll through, with any door fully open.
  3. Measure the curb from the bathroom floor to the top of the curb, and from the curb to the shower floor.
  4. Measure the bathroom door and the hallway. A shower is only as usable as the route to it.
  5. Write down where the drain is from the back wall and a side wall, and what is below the floor: basement, crawlspace, a finished ceiling or a slab.

With those five numbers you can rule sizes in or out before talking to anyone. They are also exactly what an installer will ask for on the first call.

Common questions

Shower size questions

What is the most common shower size?

Square stalls are most often 32 x 32 or 36 x 36 inches. Showers that replace a bathtub are usually 60 inches long and 30 to 32 inches deep, because that is the space a standard tub leaves.

What is the minimum shower size allowed by code?

Under IRC Section P2708.1, a shower needs at least 900 square inches of interior floor area with no finished dimension under 30 inches, such as 30 x 30. A 25 inch minimum is allowed if the floor area is at least 1,300 square inches.

Is a 36 x 36 shower big enough for a shower seat?

Yes. It is the exact inside size the 2010 ADA Standards use for a transfer shower, which includes a seat. Standing room shrinks once the seat is down, so a 48 x 36 is more comfortable if the room allows it.

What size shower does a wheelchair need?

To roll in and stay in a shower wheelchair, plan on at least 30 x 60 inches inside, the ADA standard roll-in size, with a flush or ½ inch entry. A 36 x 60 alternate roll-in gives more room to maneuver.

Can a small 32 x 32 shower be made more accessible?

Partly. Grab bars on blocked walls, a hand shower on a slide bar and a small fold-down seat help someone who can still stand and step in. It will not take a wheelchair or a walker, so a larger base is the long-term fix.
