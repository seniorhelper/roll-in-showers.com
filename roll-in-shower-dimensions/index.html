Sizes, clearances and a measuring routine

Roll-In Shower Dimensions: Measure the Whole Approach

The shower footprint is only half the answer. A roll-in shower also needs a doorway the chair fits through, room to line up at the entry, and inside space for a seat and a helper. These are the numbers, and how to measure for them.

Quick answer

Common roll-in shower sizes

The most-used reference sizes come from the 2010 ADA Standards: 30 × 60 inches inside for a standard roll-in, 36 × 60 inches for an alternate roll-in, and 36 × 36 inches for a transfer shower. Many residential pans are sold in 60 × 30 to 60 × 36 footprints because they drop into the space a bathtub used to occupy.

Outside the shower, plan on a 30 × 60-inch clear space along the entry of a standard roll-in, a doorway with at least 32 inches of clear width, and if the user turns a wheelchair in the bathroom, a 60-inch circle or a T-shaped turning space.

Two details trip people up. First, ADA inside sizes are measured at the center points of opposing walls once walls are finished, so a pan sold as "60 × 30" may have a smaller clear interior after its flanges and the wall panels are accounted for. Second, the entry width counts: a standard roll-in is open across its whole 60-inch face, while an alternate roll-in has a 36-inch opening at one end.

Not every project needs every number. A person who walks with a cane and showers seated may not need turning space, while a power wheelchair user may need more than the minimums because power chairs are often longer than manual ones. Measure the actual chair, including footrests, before you settle on a layout.

03

Step 1: Measure the route into the bathroom

Start at the bedroom or hallway the user comes from. Record each door's clear width with the door open, the hallway width at every turn, and any rug edges or thresholds along the way. A beautiful roll-in shower does little good if the chair cannot get through the bathroom door.

  • Measure clear width from the face of the door to the opposite stop, not the slab size.
  • Note which way each door swings and whether it hits a toilet or vanity.
  • Photograph each measurement with the tape visible so you can check it later.
04

Step 2: Map the room and the fixtures

Sketch the bathroom on graph paper or with painter's tape on the floor. Mark the toilet centerline, the vanity front, the radiator or heat register, the window, and the existing tub or shower. Then tape the shower footprint and the clear space in front of it. Most problems show up here: a toilet that intrudes into the entry clearance, or a vanity that blocks the turning circle.

If the user transfers to a seat, tape where the seat will be and check that the wheelchair can park beside it at the same height and angle the person normally uses.

05

Step 3: Measure the opening you are filling

For a tub replacement, measure the alcove wall to wall at the floor, at 36 inches up, and near the ceiling. Old houses are rarely square, and a ½-inch difference decides whether a 60-inch pan fits. Measure the depth from the back wall to the outside of the existing tub apron.

Then find the drain. Measure from the back wall and from one side wall to the center of the drain. Roll-in pans come with drains in fixed positions (center, end, or a trench along one edge), so this measurement narrows the product list quickly. Our pan guide explains each drain layout.

06

Step 4: Check what is under the floor

Curbless showers need the shower floor to slope toward the drain while the bathroom floor stays level at the entry. That usually means the shower area sits slightly lower than the rest of the floor, either by recessing the subfloor between joists or by building up the bathroom floor around it. Note the floor type (wood joists or concrete slab), which way the joists run if you can tell from a basement, and the total floor height you could add without creating a lip at the bathroom door.

07

Step 5: Size the inside for the person

Inside the shower, think through the routine. Where does the chair park? Which hand reaches the controls? Does a caregiver stand outside the spray or step in? A user who stays in a shower wheelchair can manage in a 60 × 30, but a caregiver helping from the side often prefers 36 inches or more of depth. A folding seat takes roughly 16 inches of depth when lowered, so it must not block the entry or the controls.

Quick fit check

If you can tape a 30 × 48-inch rectangle inside the planned shower and still reach the control location from inside it, a seated user can likely reach the valve. If not, move the valve or choose a larger footprint.

08

Write the numbers down before you shop

Put every measurement on one sheet: route widths, room sketch, opening sizes, drain location, floor type, user's chair size, seat side and control side. Hand the same sheet to every installer you call so the quotes compare like with like. When you look at a pan, compare its specification sheet to this sheet, not to the size in the product name.

Common questions

Roll-in shower dimension questions

What is the minimum size for a roll-in shower?

The 2010 ADA Standards set 30 inches wide by 60 inches deep, measured inside, for a standard roll-in shower. Homes are not required to follow that, but it is the most common benchmark for a shower a wheelchair can roll into.

Can a roll-in shower be smaller than 30 by 60?

A shower can be smaller and still be curbless, but a seated user and a shower wheelchair need room to fit. Local codes usually require at least 900 square inches inside and space for a 30-inch circle. Check the actual chair and the routine before choosing a smaller footprint.

How wide should the bathroom door be for a wheelchair?

The ADA reference is 32 inches of clear width with the door open 90 degrees. Measure clear width, not the door slab, because the stop and the door thickness reduce the opening.

Does a 60-inch tub alcove fit a roll-in shower?

Often. Many roll-in pans are made around a 60-inch length to replace a tub. Measure the alcove at several heights, confirm the drain position, and check that the bathroom has room for the chair to line up at the entry.

How much turning space does a wheelchair need?

The ADA reference is a 60-inch diameter circle or a T-shaped space within a 60-inch square. Some users can manage with less, and power chairs may need more.
