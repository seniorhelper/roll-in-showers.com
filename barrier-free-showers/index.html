Zero-entry and low-threshold showers

Barrier-Free Showers: A Practical Curbless Design Guide

"Barrier-free" means there is nothing to step over or roll over at the shower entry. Getting there takes one of three construction approaches, each with different cost, floor work and water-control tradeoffs.

Quick answer

What counts as barrier-free

A barrier-free shower has no curb. The entry is either completely flush with the bathroom floor (often called zero-entry or curbless) or has a very low, beveled lip, typically ½ inch or less. The shower floor still slopes to a drain, so the trick is lowering the shower area or raising the room around it.

Every roll-in shower is barrier-free, but not every barrier-free shower is a roll-in. A curbless 36 × 48 shower may look open and modern without being large enough for a wheelchair user and helper.

The right answer depends on the house. In a wood-framed second floor, recessing usually means reworking joists, which is a structural decision. On a slab, recessing means cutting concrete. Many tub-to-roll-in conversions use a low-profile pan because it avoids both.

02

Drains: center, linear or trench

A center (point) drain needs the floor sloped from all four sides, which works naturally with a molded pan and with small tile. A linear or trench drain runs along one edge, often the back wall or the entry, so the floor slopes in a single plane. That makes large tiles possible and lets the entry stay level across its width. Some pans offer both options; check which the listing includes.

A trench drain at the entry catches water right where it would otherwise escape, but it puts a grate under the wheels. A drain at the back wall keeps the entry clean but relies on the slope to stop water from flowing outward.

03

Slope, and why it matters more without a curb

Plumbing codes based on the International Residential Code require shower floors to slope toward the drain between ¼ inch and ½ inch per foot (2 to 4 percent). With no curb to stop overflow, the slope and drain capacity are what keep water inside. Too little slope and water creeps out; too much and a wheelchair rolls toward the drain and the floor feels uneven.

Our drainage and slope guide walks through how installers set the slope and test it.

04

Waterproofing beyond the shower

In a curbed shower the waterproofing stops at the curb. In a curbless one, some water will reach the bathroom floor, so many installers extend the waterproof membrane a few feet past the entry or across the whole bathroom floor. Ask how the membrane meets the pan or drain flange, how far it extends, and whether a flood test is planned before tile goes down.

05

Controlling splash without blocking the entry

Splash control comes from three things working together: where the showerhead points, what hangs at the entry, and how long the shower is. Aim the fixed head and hand shower away from the opening. Use a weighted curtain or a hinged glass panel that leaves the entry clear. If space allows, a longer shower (60 inches or more) keeps the wet zone far from the bathroom floor.

Tip

A collapsible water retainer, a rubber strip that folds flat under a wheel, catches most stray water at the entry and costs far less than reworking a floor.

06

Where barrier-free makes sense even without a wheelchair

Curbless showers help anyone who shuffles, uses a walker, has trouble lifting a foot, or showers seated. They are also easier to clean and leave room to add a seat or grab bars later. The NIA recommends removing tripping hazards and adding grab bars in the bathroom as people age. A low or no-threshold entry removes one of the most common bathroom obstacles.

Common questions

Barrier-free shower questions

What is the difference between barrier-free and roll-in showers?

Barrier-free describes the entry: no curb, or a very low beveled lip. Roll-in describes a shower large enough, usually at least 30 by 60 inches, for a person to roll in on a shower wheelchair.

Do curbless showers leak onto the bathroom floor?

They can if the slope, drain or splash control is wrong. A correct slope toward the drain, waterproofing that extends past the entry, and a curtain or panel that directs spray away from the opening keep water inside.

Is a trench drain better than a center drain?

Neither is better in every case. A trench drain allows a single-plane slope and large tiles; a center drain is common in molded pans. The best choice depends on the floor structure and the layout.

Can I install a curbless shower on a concrete slab?

Yes, but the slab usually has to be cut and lowered in the shower area, and the drain line under the slab may need to move. A low-profile pan can avoid most of that.
