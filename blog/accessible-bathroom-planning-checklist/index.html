Ten checks before anyone orders a product

Accessible Bathroom Planning Checklist

Most accessible bathroom problems are decided before demolition: the wrong size, a drain in the wrong place, bars without blocking. Work through these ten checks first and the rest of the project gets simpler.

Quick answer

How to use this checklist

Go through each check with the person who will use the bathroom and anyone who helps them. Write the answers on one sheet and give a copy to every installer you talk to. Use a simple three-column worksheet: known (field measurements and documented product specs), verify (concealed framing, plumbing condition, local rules, manufacturer clarifications), and decide (finishes, accessories, schedule, upgrades). That keeps preferences from being mistaken for facts.

01

Start with the person, not the product

Document how the primary user enters the room, approaches the shower, transfers or remains seated, reaches controls, manages a towel, and exits when wet. A future user may have different strength, balance, vision, or caregiver needs. Those observations turn a broad product search into a functional brief.

02

Measure the complete route

Record door widths, door swings, hallway turns, bathroom floor area, toilet and vanity projections, baseboards, finished-wall dimensions, ceiling height, drain location, supply-wall location, and subfloor type. Photograph a tape measure in each image so the scale remains clear later.

03

Separate shell size from usable space

A model name often describes nominal dimensions. Finished interior space can be smaller, while flanges and framing requirements can make the rough opening different. Obtain the current specification sheet and installation instructions for the exact model and drain orientation before demolition.

04

Treat water management as a system

A low entry changes how water is contained. Floor slope, drain capacity, waterproofing transitions, spray direction, curtain or glass placement, room ventilation, and the drying routine must work together. A powerful showerhead aimed at an open entry can defeat an otherwise careful design.

05

Coordinate structure before finishes

Grab bars, folding seats, doors, wall panels, and some controls need solid backing or manufacturer-approved anchors. Plan reinforcement while walls are open. Mark its dimensions and photograph it before the finished surface hides the framing.

06

Choose controls by reach and use

A valve that looks centered may be unreachable from a seat or outside the spray. Consider whether the user must start the water before entering, adjust temperature while seated, or hand a spray wand to a caregiver. Hose length, docking height, and control force matter.

07

Build an itemized budget

Ask the quote to separate the shower system, freight, demolition, disposal, carpentry, subfloor repair, plumbing, electrical work, waterproofing, wall finish, accessories, permits, and cleanup. Allow a contingency for concealed damage but require approval before added work.

08

Verify responsibilities in writing

Clarify who measures, orders, receives, inspects for freight damage, pulls permits, protects the home, shuts off water, handles debris, performs each trade, and completes warranty registration. Product warranty and labor warranty are separate promises.

09

Plan for maintenance

Ask how the drain is accessed, which cleaners are allowed, how seams are inspected, whether caulk is part of routine maintenance, how a folding seat is tightened, and where replacement parts come from. Easy maintenance protects both appearance and performance.

10

Use standards carefully

The ADA Standards govern covered facilities and define specific configurations. A private home is not automatically required to follow the ADA, and a single product cannot make an entire room compliant. Even so, the standards can be a valuable reference when a qualified designer applies them to the correct setting.

Common questions

Checklist questions

What should I plan before an accessible bathroom remodel?

The user's routine, the route into the room, measurements, drain location, water management, blocking for bars and seats, control placement, an itemized budget and written responsibilities.

Who should be involved in planning?

The person who will use the bathroom, anyone who helps them, the installer and, ideally, an occupational therapist.

Does a home bathroom have to meet ADA rules?

Private homes are generally not covered by the ADA Standards, but the standards are a useful reference for sizes and heights.
