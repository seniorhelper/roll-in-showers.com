How water stays in a shower with no curb

Curbless Shower Drainage, Slope and Splash Control

Without a curb, three things keep water in the shower: the slope of the floor, the drain that collects it, and the way the spray is aimed. Here is how each one works and how installers check it.

Quick answer

The numbers that matter

Codes based on the International Residential Code require a shower floor to slope toward the drain at ¼ to ½ inch per foot (2 to 4 percent). Over a 5-foot run from the entry to a drain at the far end, that is about 1¼ to 2½ inches of fall. The entry itself should stay flat or have a beveled lip of ½ inch or less for wheelchair use, so the shower floor has to start lower than the bathroom floor or be built up around it.

01

Doing the slope math

Fall equals slope times distance. At ¼ inch per foot, a 5-foot run drops 1¼ inches. A molded pan has the slope built in, so the installer's job is to set the pan perfectly level on a flat, solid base. A site-built tile floor gets its slope from a mortar bed or a pre-sloped foam tray, and the installer checks it with a level and straightedge before waterproofing.

02

Point drains vs linear drains

A point (center) drain is the familiar round grate. The floor slopes toward it from every side, which forms shallow valleys. That is fine with small tile or a molded pan, but large tile has to be cut along the slope lines. A linear or trench drain is a long, narrow channel. The floor slopes in one plane toward it, so large tiles can run straight and the entry edge can be perfectly level.

Drain capacity matters too. Check the drain's flow rating against the showerhead and hand shower, especially if two outlets can run at once.

03

Where to put the drain

Putting a linear drain at the entry catches water before it reaches the bathroom floor, and the rest of the shower floor slopes toward the opening. Wheelchair users roll over the grate, so choose a grate with narrow slots. A drain at the back wall keeps the entry clear of grates and puts the lowest point away from the room, but relies on the slope alone at the opening. Many curbless designs pair a back-wall drain with a slight secondary slope or a collapsible water retainer at the entry.

04

Recessing the floor

To keep the entry flush, the shower area usually starts lower than the bathroom floor. On wood framing, that may mean lowering the subfloor between joists or sistering and dropping joists, which is a structural change that should be planned by someone qualified. On a concrete slab, the slab is cut and the area re-poured lower. If neither is practical, a low-profile pan with a tapered entry is the usual retrofit.

05

Waterproofing and the flood test

In a curbless shower, the waterproof layer should be continuous from the drain, across the floor, up the walls, and past the entry. Many installers extend the membrane at least a few feet into the bathroom. Before tile goes down, a flood test (plugging the drain and filling the pan area with water for a period the membrane maker specifies, often 24 hours) shows whether there are leaks. Ask for a photo of the test.

06

Splash control without a door

  • Point the fixed showerhead toward the back or side wall, never toward the entry.
  • Mount the hand shower slide bar away from the opening.
  • Use a weighted-hem curtain on a rod that clears the entry, or a short fixed glass panel at the far end.
  • Add a collapsible rubber water retainer at the entry if water still creeps out.
  • Pick a bath mat that does not catch wheels, or skip it and use a towel.

07

Maintenance

Clean linear drain channels and hair traps regularly; a slow drain shows up as water at the entry. Check caulk where the pan meets the wall once or twice a year. If water pools in one spot after the shower, the slope may have settled and should be looked at before it causes damage.

Common questions

Drainage questions

How much slope does a curbless shower floor need?

Codes based on the International Residential Code require ¼ inch to ½ inch per foot toward the drain, which is 2 to 4 percent. Check the code adopted where you live.

Is a linear drain better for a roll-in shower?

A linear drain lets the floor slope in one plane and keeps the entry edge level, which suits many roll-in designs. A center drain works well with molded pans and small tile.

How do you stop water leaving a curbless shower?

Correct slope, a drain sized for the flow, spray aimed away from the opening, a weighted curtain or partial glass panel, and waterproofing that extends past the entry.

What is a shower flood test?

The drain is plugged and the waterproofed area is filled with water for the time the membrane maker specifies to check for leaks before tile or finishes go down.
