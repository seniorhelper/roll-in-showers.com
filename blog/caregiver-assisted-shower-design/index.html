Caregiver-Assisted Shower Design: Space, Reach and Dignity

When one person helps another bathe, the shower has to work for two bodies: one seated and wet, one standing and trying to stay dry. A roll-in layout makes that possible if the space, the equipment and the routine are planned together.

Quick answer

What a caregiver needs from the shower

A caregiver needs to stand outside the spray but within arm's reach, to hold the hand shower, to see and adjust water temperature, and to have supplies ready without leaving the person alone. That usually means an open-sided roll-in shower, a hand shower on a long hose, a curtain instead of a door, and a shower chair the caregiver can position and lock.

01

Where the caregiver stands

In a standard 60-inch roll-in, the caregiver usually stands at the open side, outside the wet zone, facing the seated person. A compartment 36 inches deep or more gives room to reach the far side without leaning in. Keep the floor in front of the entry clear (at least 30 by 60 inches) so the caregiver can move along it. If the caregiver has back problems, a hand shower on a slide bar reduces bending.

02

Shower chair or wall seat

Assisted bathing often uses a shower commode chair: a rolling, waterproof chair with an open seat, locking casters and sometimes a tilt function. It lets the person be moved from the bedroom to the shower in one transfer, which is often safer than transferring in a wet bathroom. A wall-mounted folding seat works for people who can sit unsupported and transfer with a little help.

Medicare lists commode chairs as covered durable medical equipment when ordered by a provider for home use. Ask the person's doctor or therapist which type they recommend.

03

Water, temperature and controls

Older adults and people with nerve damage may not feel hot water quickly. Set the water heater or anti-scald valve to limit temperature, and test the water on the caregiver's wrist before it reaches the person. Put the valve where the caregiver can reach it from outside the spray. A hand shower with an on/off button lets the caregiver pause the water without walking to the valve.

04

Privacy and dignity

Being bathed by someone else is a loss of privacy, and planning for dignity makes the routine easier for both people. Keep a towel or bath blanket over the parts of the body not being washed. Let the person do whatever they can for themselves, such as washing their face or holding the hand shower. Warm the room before undressing, and keep the door closed. Keeping to the person's familiar routine and preferences, such as time of day or order of washing, also helps.

05

Supplies within reach

  • A shelf or caddy at standing reach for the caregiver and at 15 to 48 inches for the seated person
  • Two towels and a robe on a hook just outside the spray
  • Long-handled sponge and no-rinse products for days when a full shower is too tiring
  • A non-slip mat outside the entry that does not catch wheels
  • Gloves and a waterproof apron for the caregiver if needed
  • A phone or call button within reach
06

When the routine is getting harder

If the caregiver is lifting most of the person's weight, or the person is becoming afraid in the shower, it is time to reassess. Options include a mechanical lift, a tilt-in-space shower chair, a bed bath on some days, or help from a home health aide. Medicare does not cover custodial help with bathing by itself, but home health benefits can include aide visits for people who qualify for skilled care. Talk with the person's doctor.

Common questions

Caregiver questions

What kind of shower is best for caregiver-assisted bathing?

An open-sided roll-in shower with a hand shower on a long hose, a curtain instead of a door, and room outside the entry for the caregiver to stand.

Is a shower commode chair better than a shower seat?

For people who need a lot of help, a rolling shower commode chair can be safer because it reduces transfers in the wet bathroom. A wall seat works for people who sit well and need less help.

How hot should shower water be for an older adult?

Many guides recommend keeping hot water at or below 120°F to reduce scald risk, which is also the ADA limit for shower water. Test the water before it reaches the person.

Does Medicare pay for help with bathing?

Medicare does not cover custodial care such as bathing help when that is the only care needed. Home health benefits can include aide services for people who also qualify for skilled care.
