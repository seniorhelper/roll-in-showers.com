Replacing a bathtub with a roll-in shower

Tub-to-Shower Conversion for a Lower, Easier Entry

The standard 60-inch bathtub alcove is the most common place a roll-in shower goes. The footprint is already there; the work is in the floor, the drain and the walls.

Quick answer

Why tubs convert well

Many bathtubs are about 60 inches long and 30 to 32 inches wide, which lines up with the 60-inch length of the ADA standard roll-in reference. Removing the tub wall, the 14- to 20-inch obstacle people step over, is often the single biggest bathroom safety change for someone who uses a walker or wheelchair. This Old House reports $2,000 to $12,000 for typical tub-to-shower conversions; curbless roll-ins tend to sit toward the higher end.

01

Measuring the alcove

Measure wall to wall at the floor, mid-height and near the ceiling; older walls are often out of square. Measure from the back wall to the front edge of the tub. Note the drain position (usually at one end of a tub, while many roll-in pans have center drains) and the valve height. Compare those numbers to the specification sheet of the pan you are considering.

02

The drain: left, right or center

A tub drain sits at the head end. A roll-in pan may have a center, end or trench drain. If the pan's drain does not land where the tub drain was, the drain line has to be moved. On a wood floor with access from below that is routine plumbing; on a slab it means cutting concrete. Choosing a pan with an end drain, or a trench drain that runs along the back, can avoid the move.

03

Getting a low or flush entry

A tub sits on the subfloor, so a pan dropped in its place will be slightly above the bathroom floor unless the floor is adjusted. Options are a low-profile pan with a beveled entry, recessing the subfloor in the alcove, or raising the bathroom floor. See barrier-free construction options for the tradeoffs.

04

Valve, hand shower and walls

A tub valve is often mounted low and in the middle of the end wall, where a seated person cannot easily reach it without getting sprayed. Conversion is a good time to move it toward the entry side and add a hand shower on a slide bar. Wall surfaces around a tub often stop at about 5 to 6 feet; a shower needs waterproof walls to the full height of the spray. Panel kits cover this quickly.

05

Should you keep a tub somewhere?

Some households worry that removing the only tub will hurt resale or that small children need one. If the home has another bathroom with a tub, converting one is usually an easy decision. If not, weigh the user's needs now against those concerns; the person's safety today usually matters more than a future buyer. A real estate agent in your area can tell you how local buyers view it.

06

Step-by-step

  • Measure and photograph the alcove, drain and valve.
  • Choose a pan that fits the alcove and lands close to the drain.
  • Decide how the entry will be made low or flush.
  • Plan blocking for bars and a seat.
  • Plan the new valve and hand shower positions.
  • Get an itemized quote that includes disposal of the tub.
  • Schedule the work and any inspection.

Common questions

Tub conversion questions

Can any bathtub be converted to a roll-in shower?

Most standard 60-inch tub alcoves can be. The drain position, floor structure and the width of the bathroom outside the alcove decide how easy it is.

How much does a tub to roll-in shower conversion cost?

This Old House reports $2,000 to $12,000 for tub-to-shower conversions. Curbless roll-ins with floor or drain work tend to cost more than a simple swap.

Will I lose resale value without a bathtub?

It depends on your market and whether the home has another tub. Ask a local real estate agent; for many households, safety now is the bigger concern.

Does the drain have to move?

Only if the new pan's drain does not line up with the tub drain. Picking a pan with a matching drain location avoids the move.
