Walk-In or Roll-In? Planning for Changing Mobility

A walk-in shower with a low curb works well for many older adults. A roll-in becomes the better choice once a walker, a shower chair or a wheelchair enters the picture, or when a caregiver starts helping. Here is how to tell which side of that line you are on.

Quick answer

The dividing line

If the person can step over a 2- to 4-inch curb safely and showers standing or on a small seat, a walk-in shower may be enough. If they use a walker in the bathroom, need a rolling shower chair, use a wheelchair, or need hands-on help, plan a roll-in: no curb, a larger footprint, and room for a chair and a helper.

This page focuses on the point where wheels come in: walkers, rolling shower chairs, wheelchairs and caregiver help.

01

Signs a walk-in shower may not be enough

  • The person holds onto towel bars or the sink to step in.
  • A walker comes into the bathroom.
  • They shower seated, or need to.
  • Someone helps with bathing, even occasionally.
  • A recent fall, surgery or diagnosis has changed balance or strength.
  • A doctor or therapist has suggested a shower chair or wheelchair.

The CDC reports that more than one in four adults 65 and older fall each year, and that falling once raises the chance of falling again. A planned bathroom is one of the practical responses.

03

Planning ahead without over-building

A roll-in shower does not have to look clinical. The footprint and the flush entry are the hard parts to change later; grab bars, a seat and a hand shower can be added when needed if blocking is in place. A practical middle path for an active older adult is a curbless or very low threshold shower, at least 60 inches long, with blocking in all walls and a slide-bar hand shower from day one.

04

Medical input

An occupational therapist can do a home safety assessment and recommend the shower setup for the person's current and expected abilities. Medicare Part B may cover therapy services when they are medically necessary and ordered by a provider; ask the person's doctor whether a home safety evaluation makes sense.

05

Paying for it

Original Medicare generally does not pay for bathroom remodels. Medicaid waivers, VA programs, Area Agencies on Aging and some Medicare Advantage plans may help; see funding an accessible bathroom.

Common questions

Mobility planning questions

Is a walk-in or roll-in shower better for seniors?

A walk-in with a low curb suits people who walk steadily. A roll-in suits people who use a walker in the bathroom, shower seated in a rolling chair, use a wheelchair, or need help bathing.

Can a walk-in shower be converted to a roll-in later?

Sometimes, but changing the curb and floor later usually means redoing the base. Building curbless with blocking in place from the start is easier.

Who can assess what shower a parent needs?

An occupational therapist can do a home safety assessment. Ask the person's doctor for a referral.
