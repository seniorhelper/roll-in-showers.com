ADA Showers: What the Standards Actually Require

The ADA Standards define three shower compartments and set exact numbers for size, entry, threshold, seat, grab bars and controls. Here is what each rule says, where it applies, and how to use it as a benchmark at home.

Quick answer

The short version of section 608

Under the 2010 ADA Standards, a standard roll-in shower is at least 30 inches wide and 60 inches deep inside, with an entry that spans the full 60-inch side. An alternate roll-in is at least 36 by 60 inches with a 36-inch entry at one end. A transfer shower is exactly 36 by 36 inches and is used from a seat, not a wheelchair. Roll-in thresholds can be no more than ½ inch high.

The ADA Standards apply to public accommodations, commercial facilities and state and local government buildings. A private single-family home is generally not covered, which is why careful contractors say a home shower is "built to ADA dimensions" rather than "ADA certified." There is no such certificate for a house.

01

Three compartment types, three different users

Section 608.2 sorts showers by how a person gets in. In a transfer shower the user parks a wheelchair outside, then slides onto a built-in seat. In both roll-in types the user stays in a shower wheelchair or rolls in and transfers to a folding seat inside. The numbers follow from those movements.

Inside sizes are measured at the center points of opposing sides. Source: 2010 ADA Standards, U.S. Access Board Chapter 6.

Notice that the transfer shower is the only type with an exact size. At 36 by 36 inches, a seated person can reach the controls and grab bars without leaning far, so a larger stall would actually make it harder to use. Roll-in compartments only have minimums, so bigger is allowed.

02

Thresholds and the floor at the entry

Section 608.7 limits the threshold in a roll-in shower to ½ inch high. A change in level up to ¼ inch can be vertical; between ¼ and ½ inch it must be beveled no steeper than 1:2, following §303. Transfer showers have the same ½-inch limit, with a beveled, rounded or vertical edge permitted.

For a roll-in, ½ inch is a ceiling, not a target. A shower wheelchair has small casters, and many users find a flush entry or a gentle ramped transition easier. The tradeoff is water control: the lower the lip, the more the floor slope, drain position and spray direction have to do. Our curbless drainage guide covers that system.

03

Seats: when they are required and where they go

Transfer showers must have a seat, folding or fixed (§608.4). In standard roll-in showers a seat is optional in most settings, but transient lodging guest rooms with mobility features need a folding seat (§608.4 with §806.2). When a seat is provided in a standard roll-in, it goes on an end wall, and the controls and grab bars are arranged around it.

Seat details are in §610. Shower seats are mounted 17 to 19 inches above the bathroom floor, measured to the top of the seat. The standard also sets seat shapes (rectangular or L-shaped), depths and the strength the seat and its fasteners must resist. At home, the best height is the one that matches the user's wheelchair seat so the transfer is level. See shower seats and benches for choosing one.

04

Grab bars: layout depends on the compartment

Grab bars must meet §609: a round gripping surface 1¼ to 2 inches in diameter, 1½ inches of space between the bar and the wall, and a horizontal mounting height of 33 to 36 inches above the floor, measured to the top of the bar.

  • Transfer shower (§608.3.1): bars across the control wall and on the back wall to a point 18 inches from the control wall.
  • Standard roll-in with a seat (§608.3.2): bars on the back wall and on the side wall opposite the seat. No bar is mounted above the seat, and bars end no more than 6 inches from the adjacent wall.
  • Standard roll-in without a seat: bars on all three walls.
  • Alternate roll-in (§608.3.3): bars on the walls surrounding the user, again with none above a seat; follow the §608.3.3 figure for the exact runs.

All bars need solid blocking or a manufacturer-approved anchor. Plan that reinforcement before the walls close, even if you install bars later.

05

Controls, hand shower and water temperature

In a standard roll-in, the controls, faucet and spray unit sit on the back wall above the grab bar and no higher than 48 inches above the shower floor. Where a seat is present, they must be within 27 inches of the seat wall (§608.5.2). In a transfer shower they go on the side wall opposite the seat, 38 to 48 inches above the floor and no more than 15 inches past the seat centerline toward the opening (§608.5.1).

Every accessible shower needs a hand-held spray on a hose at least 59 inches long that can also work as a fixed showerhead, with an on/off control that does not need to be held to stay off (§608.6). Showers must not deliver water hotter than 120°F. In a home, an anti-scald pressure-balance or thermostatic valve does the same job.

06

Enclosures and what cannot block the user

Section 608.8 says a curtain, door or glass panel must not block the controls, the spray unit, or the transfer from a wheelchair to the seat. In practice that rules out fixed glass across a roll-in entry and doors that swing into the seat. Weighted curtains, collapsible water retainers and outward-folding panels are the usual answers. See design ideas for options that still look finished.

07

Where ADA applies, and what governs a home instead

The ADA Standards cover places of public accommodation such as hotels, clinics, gyms and senior centers, along with commercial facilities and state and local government buildings. They are not a residential code. Multifamily housing built for first occupancy after March 13, 1991, is covered by the Fair Housing Act design requirements, and many building codes use ICC A117.1 for accessible dwelling units. Under A117.1, "Type A" units have bathing fixtures closer to §608, and "Type B" units mainly require wall reinforcement for future grab bars and seats.

For a private house, the controlling rules are the plumbing and building codes your city or county has adopted (often based on the International Residential Code), the manufacturer's instructions, and the needs of the person using the room. The ADA numbers are still the most widely tested reference, which is why designers borrow them.

Plain wording that stays accurate

Say "built to ADA roll-in dimensions" or "ADA-style layout." Avoid "ADA certified" or "ADA compliant home." A product label alone cannot make a room compliant, because compliance depends on the whole installation and the setting.

08

Using §608 as a home benchmark

Start with the person. A user who stays in a shower wheelchair needs roll-in proportions and controls reachable from the chair. A user who transfers to a seat can be well served by an alternate roll-in or a 36-inch transfer layout with a low threshold. A caregiver who helps from outside the spray may need more length than 60 inches.

Then check the room. The 30-by-60-inch space in front of a standard roll-in is where the wheelchair lines up before entering. If a vanity or toilet sits there, the plan may need a different compartment type or a door change. Our dimensions guide walks through measuring each of these.

Common questions

ADA shower questions

What size is an ADA roll-in shower?

A standard roll-in shower under the 2010 ADA Standards is at least 30 inches wide and 60 inches deep inside, with a 60-inch-wide entry. An alternate roll-in is at least 36 by 60 inches with a 36-inch entry at one end of the long side.

How high can an ADA roll-in shower threshold be?

Section 608.7 allows a threshold up to ½ inch high. Anything over ¼ inch must be beveled at a slope no steeper than 1:2.

Do homes have to meet ADA shower rules?

Private single-family homes are generally not covered by the ADA Standards. Local building and plumbing codes apply, and some multifamily housing must meet Fair Housing Act or ICC A117.1 requirements. Many homeowners still use the ADA dimensions as a planning benchmark.

How long does the hand shower hose need to be?

The ADA Standards require a hose at least 59 inches long on a spray unit that works both as a fixed showerhead and as a hand-held shower.

Where do grab bars go in a roll-in shower with a seat?

On the back wall and the side wall opposite the seat, 33 to 36 inches above the floor. Bars are not mounted above the seat.
