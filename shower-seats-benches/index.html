Shower Seats and Benches: Size, Support and Placement

The right seat makes a roll-in shower usable for someone who cannot stand for long, and a safe landing point for a transfer. The wrong seat blocks the entry, sits at the wrong height or pulls out of the wall.

Quick answer

Seat types at a glance

Wall-mounted folding seats flip up out of the way and are the usual choice in roll-in showers. Fixed or built-in benches suit transfer showers and people who always sit. Shower chairs and shower commode chairs are freestanding or rolling and need no wall work. Transfer benches straddle a tub or entry and are mainly a stopgap.

02

Height and depth

The ADA reference height for shower seats is 17 to 19 inches above the bathroom floor, measured to the top of the seat. Rectangular seats in the ADA Standards are about 15 to 16 inches deep from the wall. For home use, match the seat to the user: measure their wheelchair seat height with the cushion and set the shower seat to the same height for a level transfer. Taller users may prefer a slightly higher seat; shorter users need their feet flat on the floor.

03

Where the seat goes

In a standard roll-in, the seat goes on an end wall, the controls on the back wall within reach of the seat, and grab bars on the back wall and the wall opposite the seat (not above it). The seat must not block the entry when lowered. In a transfer shower, the seat is on the wall opposite the controls and extends the full depth of the stall.

Think about the person's stronger side. The seat should be placed so the chair can park on that side.

04

Strength and blocking

Seats carry a person's full weight, sometimes with a sudden drop. The ADA requires seats and their fasteners to resist 250 pounds of force; bariatric users need seats rated higher. Fasten wall seats into solid blocking or a backing system approved by the seat maker, never into drywall or panel alone. Photograph the blocking before the walls close.

05

Materials

Look for seats with drainage slots, rust-resistant hardware, and a surface that is not slippery when wet but is easy to clean. Teak and phenolic seats are common; padded seats are more comfortable but need more cleaning. Check that the folded seat does not catch a shoulder or a caregiver's hip.

Common questions

Seat questions

How high should a shower seat be?

The ADA reference is 17 to 19 inches from the floor to the top of the seat. At home, match it to the user's wheelchair seat height for a level transfer.

Is a folding shower seat strong enough?

A properly rated seat fastened into solid blocking is. The ADA requires seats and fasteners to resist 250 pounds; choose a higher rating for bariatric users.

Do I need a seat in a roll-in shower?

Not always. Users who stay in a shower wheelchair may not use one. A folding seat adds flexibility for other users and future needs.

Will Medicare pay for a shower chair?

Medicare lists commode chairs as covered durable medical equipment when a provider orders them. Ask the person's provider which equipment is appropriate.
